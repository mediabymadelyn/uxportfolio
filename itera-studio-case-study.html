Overview

Itera Studio is an AI-assisted reference search for artists. It returns real, credited artworks and photographs from open collections instead of generating images or training on artist work. My senior comprehensive project at Occidental College began by asking whether AI could help artists find references in a way that feels helpful rather than intrusive. I designed and built the system, conducted the research, ran a 10-person user study, and developed it into a live web app.

Attribution & Licensing by Design

Credit and licensing are part of how Itera works, not something added on top.

  • Only public-domain or openly licensed works are shown.
  • Every card shows the creator, source, license, and a link to the original.
  • Users can export credits and print reference sheets.
  • No image generation, and no training on artist work.
  • Search text is not stored, and cache keys are SHA-256 hashes.
  • No account is required.

Where It Started

Itera's precursor was DoodleQuest, which I built in COMP181 during my freshman year (2023–24). It was a static Java quiz that mapped user responses to a fixed set of 20 prompts. The core idea was right: artists need targeted, growth-oriented challenges rather than generic ones. But the execution was too rigid and the tool was doing all the thinking for the user.

In Spring 2025 I ran an independent study with Professor Sanchez focused on AI prompting and illustration. Over the semester I created five bodies of work responding to ChatGPT prompts across gesture and composition, color theory, self-expression, digital art, and mixed media. I was not just making art. I was evaluating how AI interacted with creative process, and documenting what worked, what did not, and why.

One of the main findings was that AI prompts skew Eurocentric by default. When I asked for gesture drawing prompts, the suggestions leaned toward yoga poses. Color theory suggestions reflected Western associations with "warmth" and "calm" without cultural nuance. These are not neutral failures. They are baked-in biases. I developed a six-category rubric for evaluating prompts on creativity, flexibility, clarity, accessibility and inclusivity, engagement, and excitement, and used it to assess how AI performed across different creative contexts. The full write-up is on my Writing page.

The clearest finding: a good prompt does not tell you what to make. It helps you ask better questions.

User Research and the Pivot

Before building further, I needed to know whether artists actually wanted AI critique tools, so I ran two separate surveys.

The first was a small UX survey (n=4). Four participants looked at my Figma prototype, tested existing AI critique tools, and rated the AI feedback on a Likert scale. With only four people, these are counts, not trends:

  • 3 of 4agreed the feedback was helpful for improving their work.
  • 3 of 4agreed the feedback felt personalized to their artwork.
  • 2 of 4agreed the feedback gave clear, actionable next steps.
  • 2 of 4disagreed that AI feedback made them feel more confident.
  • 3.75/5average overall satisfaction.
What participants wrote

The open-ended responses were more telling. One participant wrote that they could not tell what the AI was referring to when it suggested changes to proportions. Another wanted exercises that fit their specific style, not generic corrections. One said they would take human critique again, but only from someone who understood what they needed and could apply it with confidence in mind.

The second was a separate, open-ended survey I posted to art communities on Reddit and Discord, which reached a larger group of artists. Those responses were more pointed, and overwhelmingly skeptical of AI critique tools, with concerns around ethics, trust, and the perception that AI cannot genuinely understand visual intention. One artist captured what would become the direction of Itera:

"The only real purpose I can see AI being useful for as a tool is an AI that finds specific real reference images based on a prompt. Every artist struggles to find the perfect reference image at specific perspective angles and often spends hours searching instead of doing art."

That response was not an outlier. It was a pattern. Artists did not want AI to evaluate their work. They wanted help finding real work to study.

The pivot was direct: away from critique, toward reference discovery. The role of AI shifted from judge to navigator.

What Itera Does

Itera is designed around one idea: connect artists to real references from real artists, with attribution intact.

A user enters a natural language description of what they are trying to make. The system interprets that intent, expands it into related visual concepts and terms, and routes the query across multiple databases simultaneously. Results come back with artist credit, source link, and license information so the user knows exactly what they are looking at and where it came from.

The search interface shows the interpreted query alongside AI-expanded tags so the user can see how their intent was understood and adjust it. Filters for refining and exploring similar results keep the user in control of the direction.

Step 1: Interpret Search

  • The AI analyzes natural language input to understand artistic intent.
  • Conceptual language, mood, and medium are parsed into structured terms before any query is run.

Step 2: Expand Intent

  • The system generates related visual concepts and search terms from the interpreted intent.
  • The query is widened without overriding the user's direction, surfacing adjacent ideas they may not have named.

Step 3: Surface Results

  • References are returned from verified databases with artist credit, source link, and license information attached.
  • Duplicates across sources are removed. Results are ranked by relevance, metadata quality, and source trust.

Building Itera

Itera is built with Next.js and TypeScript. It searches the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, and Unsplash, normalizing their results into one shared schema. Every card shows the work or photograph, creator, source, license, and an original-source link.

Llama 3.3 70B turns natural-language searches into a structured plan, while a rule-based fallback keeps search working if the model fails. sharp contributes pixel-level analysis, Upstash Redis caches results, and museum catalog terms are translated into the language used by each collection. AI does not generate or train on images: it interprets the search and helps rank and describe real candidate works.

Museum catalog data rarely records the visual qualities artists search for. A metadata-gap experiment on 38 museum works (21 from the Met and 17 from the Art Institute of Chicago) found that composition and mood tags could be described reliably, while lighting was dependable only when the vision model reported confidence. The labels for those works were created by AI, and I have been checking them iteratively and interviewing people about the output.

Itera later used Gemini 3.8 Flash to inspect up to 40 candidates per search and return structured match, lighting, color, and label data. In a model comparison on 40 test images, each judged twice by each model, Gemini scored 31/32 on relevance vs. 24/32 for Gemma, and incorrectly called flat images dramatic 3/16 times vs. 6/16 for Gemma. Relevance and lighting were each scored only on the images labeled for that test.

Search quality

  • Best matches contain at most one work per artist and at least three museum works.
  • Top matches are checked for duplicates and ranked from visible visual qualities.
  • Each candidate can receive more than one descriptive label.

Artist tools

  • Values view, mirror, grid overlay, and main-color extraction support reference study.
  • Collections and notes stay in the browser, alongside filters and export tools.
  • Users can generate credits exports and printable reference sheets.

Privacy and ethics

  • Only public-domain or openly licensed works are shown.
  • Search text is not stored, and cache keys are SHA-256 hashes.
  • No account is required; saved material remains in the user's browser.

Testing With Artists

Ten artists completed approximately 30-minute, recorded think-aloud sessions. Each participant completed three set searches, an open-ended illustration task, and the System Usability Scale survey. Eight participants completed SUS, producing a score of 87.5/100, in the “excellent” range and above the industry average of 68.

  • 87.5/100System Usability Scale score.
  • 8/10found source filtering useful.
  • 7/10found the ethical approach clear and acceptable.
  • 6/10said credits and licenses were visible enough.

The first round also surfaced important failures. Eight participants rejected the top result for a dramatic lighting search. At that stage, Itera ranked candidates from titles and catalog data rather than image analysis, which could place an unrelated work at the top. Participants also asked for more labels, more results and mediums, and noticed gender and cultural skew. A few quotes captured the project's potential: “I would use this instead of Google,” and “A strong alternative for Pinterest.”

What changed after testing

Itera now runs Gemini's vision check across candidate images, returns confidence-based lighting information, and uses a fixed lighting score for more consistent ranking. The redesign also added a public-domain artwork palette, collections, notes, filter backup and restore, a credits export, and printable reference sheets.

What Comes Next

  • Run a second round of artist testing with the vision-based ranking and redesigned interface.
  • Add new sources one at a time, beginning with the Cleveland Museum of Art, then Smithsonian Open Access and the Rijksmuseum.
  • Build “more like this” image search for style and visual qualities across the catalog.
  • Keep expanding evaluation checks for license, provenance, diversity, and ranking quality.

Reflection

Itera taught me that AI can be useful in creative work without taking over the creative process. The model works best on narrow, visible judgments; the surrounding system determines trust, provenance, fallback, and control.

The most important result came from testing the system between phases, not from a benchmark alone. When Itera ranked from catalog text, artists rejected the top choices. Adding a visual check fixed a real user problem and made the ethical premise more credible: AI could explain and compare references, but only when it could actually see them.

I also learned to separate research findings from product claims. A reliable result across 38 museum works, with labels created by AI that I have been checking iteratively, is not the same as a universal ranking system, and a strong SUS score does not eliminate cultural or gender skew. Itera now documents these limits, preserves user control, and treats new sources and models as things that require separate validation.
